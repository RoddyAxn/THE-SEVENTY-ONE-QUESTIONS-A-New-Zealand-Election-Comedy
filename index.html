The Seventy-One Questions

A New Zealand Election Comedy in Several Acts
In the manner of Shakespeare, Marlowe, and Billy T. James
By Roderick J. Young
26 September 2026

Dramatis Personae

NEIL DENBY — an independent candidate with a telephone, an electorate, and considerably more optimism than time.

RODERICK — writer, organiser, collector of questions, recorders, badges and improbable political possibilities.

KARL BARKLEY — engineer, farmer, political organiser and believer that seventy-one independent candidates might yet be found.

CLIVE — Neil’s cousin, somewhere beyond the political battlefield, suspected of knowing half the South Island.

UNCLE KEVIN MARKS — eighty years old, sharp of mind, unexpectedly invited into the political arena.

JACKSON — twenty-seven, fence painter, water-blaster, worker in mysterious new sprayed coatings, and equally unexpectedly invited into politics.

STEVE HART — independent campaigner, armed with a public meeting and an audio recorder.

JACK — wearer of the badge.

ROSETTA — observer of all things labelled “ONE TICK INDEPENDENT.”

THE FOUR PARTY REPRESENTATIVES — travellers from the Established Kingdoms.

THE PARTY WHIP — a mysterious driver.

THE BULL — representing that which political language sometimes fails to notice until rather late.

THE CHORUS OF SEVENTY-ONE QUESTIONS — persistent, inconvenient and extremely difficult to filibuster.

THE AUDIENCE — sovereign citizens, amateur constitutional scholars, professional hecklers and people who only came because the hall had biscuits.

Prologue

Enter RODERICK with seventy-one sheets of paper.

RODERICK:
Attend, good folk of Aotearoa’s shore,
Where seventy-one electorates stand,
And parties marshal banners as of yore
While independents wander through the land.

We have no castle.

We have no army.

We have no great campaign headquarters filled with young persons drinking coffee and saying “stakeholder engagement.”

We have—

He searches his pockets.

—a cellphone.

A badge.

A Word document.

And seventy-one questions.

Enter CHORUS.

CHORUS: Seventy-one!

RODERICK: Yes, thank you.

CHORUS: SEVENTY-ONE!

RODERICK: You are going to do that all night, aren't you?

CHORUS: Possibly.

RODERICK: Then let the experiment commence.

Act I

The Letter to Clive

NEIL sits at a table writing.

NEIL: “Hi Clive.”

He stops.

Too informal?

RODERICK: No.

NEIL: “Dear Sir and Most Esteemed Cousin,
Whose Wisdom Lights Dunedin like the sun—”

RODERICK: Too formal.

NEIL: Right.

“Hi Clive.”

He writes.

Attached herein, good cousin, lies our scheme:
One Tick Independent is the theme.

If thou believest party politics
Hath reached a somewhat complicated fix—

RODERICK: You said it was dead.

NEIL: I was being diplomatic.

If thou believest party rule is dead,
Then action must arise in words’ stead.

We seek good women, good men too,
And anyone else prepared to stand up and answer a question without consulting fourteen pages of party policy.

RODERICK: Excellent.

NEIL: Some say that I have wholly lost my mind.

RODERICK: What do you say?

NEIL: I say they may be right but we're short of candidates.

Audience laughs.

NEIL: Someone must attempt it.

And if good people merely stand aside—

RODERICK: —then people less good may get the job.

NEIL: Precisely.

Dunedin! Christchurch! Invercargill!

Let every southern hill and valley ring.

RODERICK: Careful. We haven't paid for a sound system.

NEIL: Clive, thou mayest know a person there
Who secretly hath thought:

“By crikey, Parliament couldn't possibly be that difficult.”

RODERICK: Many have thought this.

NEIL: Then send them forth!

Tell them:

Here is the vehicle.

Get in touch.

RODERICK: Does the vehicle have a warrant?

NEIL: Metaphorically.

RODERICK: Good.

NEIL: And cousin—

It hath been nearly half a mortal life
Since we two met.

May health attend thee.

Pass this onward if thou wilt.

All the best,

Neil.

He presses SEND.

NEIL: There.

RODERICK: How many candidates did that get us?

NEIL: I've only just sent it!

Act II

Too Old and Too Young

Enter UNCLE KEVIN, aged eighty.

RODERICK: Uncle Kevin!

KEVIN: What now?

RODERICK: Would you stand for Parliament?

KEVIN: I'm eighty.

RODERICK: Excellent life experience.

KEVIN: My knees have life experience too.

RODERICK: There's a Māori electorate in Auckland—

KEVIN: Rod.

RODERICK: Yes?

KEVIN: I'm eighty.

RODERICK: I heard you the first time.

KEVIN: Good.

Now explain this One Tick thing.

RODERICK: An independent contests the electorate vote.

KEVIN: And the party vote?

RODERICK: The voter decides that separately.

KEVIN: So you're not telling them which party to vote for?

RODERICK: Correct.

KEVIN: Hmmm.

And what are you asking candidates?

RODERICK: First question:

“Do you support reinstatement of oil-refining capability at Marsden Point?”

KEVIN: That makes good sense to ask.

Who are you voting for?

RODERICK: That, Uncle Kevin, is precisely the question every voter must answer for themselves.

KEVIN: Good answer.

Still not standing.

Exit KEVIN. Enter JACKSON carrying a water blaster.

JACKSON: G'day.

RODERICK: Jackson! Twenty-seven years old!

JACKSON: That's what the birth certificate says.

RODERICK: Would you stand for Parliament?

JACKSON: I paint fences.

RODERICK: Parliament has walls.

JACKSON: I water-blast fences.

RODERICK: Parliament could probably use that too.

JACKSON: And we've got this new paint system. Spray it on—

RODERICK: Splendid! Economic development!

JACKSON: Mate, I'm trying to explain paint.

RODERICK: And I am trying to recruit a candidate.

JACKSON: I'm twenty-seven.

RODERICK: Too young?

JACKSON: No.

Too busy.

Exit JACKSON.

RODERICK: Too old.

Too young.

Somewhere between eighty and twenty-seven—

Enter KARL.

KARL: The sweet spot!

RODERICK: Precisely.

Act III

The Sweet Spot

KARL: There lies a moment in the life of men
When madness masquerades as possibility.

RODERICK: That's Marlowe.

KARL: Good line though.

RODERICK: Continue.

KARL: The parties have organisation.

Money.

Databases.

Signs.

Candidates.

Volunteers.

Media teams.

Research units.

RODERICK: And we have?

KARL raises a small badge.

KARL: This.

RODERICK: Ah.

KARL: Never underestimate the button badge.

Empires have fallen for less.

RODERICK: I don't think they have.

KARL: Never let historical accuracy ruin a campaign anecdote.

Enter JACK wearing a tiny badge reading: ONE TICK INDEPENDENT. ROSETTA enters.

ROSETTA: What's that?

JACK: One Tick Independent.

ROSETTA: What's it mean?

All freeze.

RODERICK: Aha!

The first victory.

Not agreement.

Not disagreement.

Curiosity.

ROSETTA: I said what's it mean.

RODERICK: Exactly!

If a badge raises a question,
The conversation hath begun.

JACK: And if someone photographs me?

RODERICK: Front page.

Small badge.

Large idea.

ROSETTA: Or nobody notices.

RODERICK: That is also possible.

ROSETTA: Good.

Just checking reality was still invited.

Act IV

Steve Hart Meets the Establishment

A community hall. Eighty chairs. A tea urn. Four PARTY REPRESENTATIVES. STEVE HART stands before them.

STEVE: Welcome.

FIRST PARTY REPRESENTATIVE: Where are your people?

STEVE: You're looking at them.

SECOND PARTY REPRESENTATIVE: There aren't many.

STEVE: Yet.

THIRD PARTY REPRESENTATIVE: We have branches throughout the country.

STEVE: Very good.

FOURTH PARTY REPRESENTATIVE: We have parliamentary research staff.

STEVE: Also very good.

FIRST PARTY REPRESENTATIVE: What have you got?

Steve holds up his cellphone.

STEVE: Flight mode.

Plenty of storage.

Forty-eight percent battery.

And I'm recording everything.

RODERICK: From now on, record your appearances!

Meetings. Candidate forums. Speeches. Questions. Answers.

Audio on your cellphone.

Preserve it. Transcribe it. Put it on the website. Turn speeches into articles.

Let the candidate's own words survive the evening.

STEVE: What if I give a terrible speech?

RODERICK: Then history deserves accuracy.

Act V

The Great Book of Questions

Thunder. A vast book descends. On its cover: 71 CONSCIENCE QUESTIONS.

CHORUS: SEVENTY-ONE!

RODERICK: Quiet!

CHORUS: Sorry.

NEIL: Why seventy-one?

RODERICK: Because there are seventy-one electorates in the proposal.

KARL: And because seventy-two would look excessive.

RODERICK: Each question should expose a choice.

Clear enough that a voter can understand the issue.

Fair enough that opposing answers are permitted.

Specific enough that “I support outcomes-based strategic stakeholder conversations” won't answer it.

STEVE: Example?

RODERICK: Question One:

Do you support investigating the restoration of oil-refining capability at Marsden Point?

Yes or no?

PARTY REPRESENTATIVE: Well, our party's position acknowledges the complex interplay between international energy markets—

CHORUS: YES OR NO?

PARTY REPRESENTATIVE: The international rules-based—

CHORUS: YES. OR. NO?

PARTY REPRESENTATIVE: It's more nuanced—

RODERICK: Nuance may follow.

But first:

Where do you stand?

Act VI

The Seventy Questions Yet Unasked

NEIL: And what of the remaining seventy?

RODERICK: They must not become a catechism in which one answer is declared virtuous and another damned.

Let them instead reveal where candidates stand.

KARL: Give us examples.

RODERICK opens the book.

2. Should New Zealand hold larger strategic reserves of essential fuels?

3. Should major infrastructure proposals be subject to published long-term cost-benefit assessments?

4. Should electorate MPs publish how they vote on major conscience issues?

5. Should MPs be free to vote against their party on specified issues without disciplinary consequences?

6. Should citizens have greater use of binding referendums, and if so, under what safeguards?

7. Should local government funding rely less heavily on property rates?

8. Should GST treatment of basic necessities be reviewed?

9. Should New Zealand increase investment in rail freight infrastructure?

10. Should major foreign ownership of strategic infrastructure face additional scrutiny?

11. Should government departments publish more information proactively rather than waiting for Official Information Act requests?

12. Should parliamentary select committees have stronger powers to compel evidence?

13. Should New Zealand's electricity market structure be independently reviewed?

14. Should schools place greater emphasis on civics and practical understanding of government?

15. Should vocational and trade education receive funding treatment comparable with university pathways?

16. Should New Zealand maintain domestic capacity to manufacture or repair strategically important equipment?

17. Should elected representatives disclose more detail about lobbying contacts?

18. Should councils have greater flexibility to permit small dwellings and housing intensification?

19. Should long-term infrastructure planning extend beyond the three-year electoral cycle?

20. Should major emergency supplies be maintained domestically rather than relying principally on just-in-time international supply chains?

NEIL: Stop!

RODERICK: There are fifty-one left.

NEIL: Not tonight.

RODERICK: Excellent.

That means the audience returns for the sequel.

Act VII

The Bull, the Windscreen and the Whip

A motorcar appears. THE PARTY WHIP is driving. A PARTY CANDIDATE sits beside him holding a 486-page manifesto.

CANDIDATE: I cannot see the road.

WHIP: Why?

CANDIDATE: The policy document covers the windscreen.

WHIP: Never mind.

The party research unit says the highway remains broadly consistent with expectations.

A BULL wanders onto the road.

CANDIDATE: What's that?

WHIP: Our polling says no significant bovine issue exists.

CANDIDATE: There's a bull!

WHIP: That would require caucus discussion.

CANDIDATE: BRAKE!

WHIP: Our policy is to evaluate braking options after consultation with stakeholders.

CANDIDATE: THE BULL!

WHIP: We cannot make policy on the hoof.

BULL: Moooo.

CANDIDATE: One hundred and ten kilometres an hour!

WHIP: Do not worry.

The communications team has prepared key messages.

CRASH. Silence. The BULL strolls off unharmed.

BULL: Moooo.

RODERICK: And thus sometimes does elaborate language meet reality.

NEIL: What became of the candidate?

RODERICK: Promoted.

NEIL: And the whip?

RODERICK: Appointed spokesperson for transport.

Act VIII

The Public Meeting

A lectern. Candidates sit in a row. The audience waits.

CHAIR: Questions from the floor.

RODERICK rises.

RODERICK: Question One.

Do you support investigating the restoration of oil-refining capability at Marsden Point?

Candidate One?

CANDIDATE ONE: Yes.

RODERICK: Thank you.

Sits down.

CANDIDATE ONE: Is that it?

RODERICK: Yes.

CANDIDATE ONE: You don't want a five-minute speech?

RODERICK: No.

CANDIDATE ONE: A rebuttal?

RODERICK: No.

CANDIDATE ONE: Attack my opponent?

RODERICK: Definitely no.

CANDIDATE ONE: This is unsettling.

RODERICK: Candidate Two?

CANDIDATE TWO: No.

RODERICK: Thank you.

Sits.

CANDIDATE TWO: You're not going to argue?

RODERICK: No.

The answer is information.

The voter may decide what weight to give it.

CANDIDATE THREE: What if the answer is complicated?

RODERICK: Then say:

“Yes, because—”

or:

“No, because—”

or:

“I cannot responsibly answer yes or no because—”

But do not hide the conclusion beneath a forest of words.

OLD MAN IN AUDIENCE: Hear, hear!

YOUNG WOMAN IN AUDIENCE: Can we ask the independents the same questions?

Silence.

RODERICK: Absolutely.

YOUNG WOMAN: All seventy-one?

RODERICK: Every one.

KARL: That was not in the plan.

RODERICK: It is now.

Act IX

Conscience

Night. The hall is empty. RODERICK, NEIL, KARL and STEVE remain.

STEVE: What if our own candidates disagree?

RODERICK: Then they disagree.

KARL: But how can seventy-one independents work together if each has a conscience?

NEIL: Perhaps that is the experiment.

Not seventy-one identical voices—

but seventy-one representatives required to explain themselves.

STEVE: What holds them together?

NEIL: Questions.

Transparency.

Promises made publicly.

Votes later compared with those promises.

RODERICK: And the electorate watching.

KARL: No whip?

RODERICK: Conscience is a difficult animal to whip.

NEIL: And suppose the independent is wrong?

RODERICK: Then democracy supplies another ancient instrument.

STEVE: What's that?

RODERICK: The next election.

Act X

The Madness of Karl, Neil and Roderick

KARL: Seventy-one candidates!

NEIL: Weeks remaining!

STEVE: Small meetings!

JACK: Tiny badges!

JACKSON: Fence paint!

KEVIN: I'm still not standing!

ROSETTA: And half the country hasn't heard of you.

RODERICK: All true.

KARL: Then are we mad?

NEIL: Possibly.

STEVE: Deluded?

RODERICK: Occasionally.

JACK: Underfunded?

ALL: YES!

RODERICK: At last—a unanimous conscience vote.

Act XI

Enter the Ghost of Marlowe

Smoke.

MARLOWE'S GHOST: Was this the face that launch'd a thousand independents?

RODERICK: No.

That's Jack.

He's wearing the badge.

MARLOWE'S GHOST: Ah.

I had expected something more dramatic.

JACK: It's only twenty-five millimetres across.

MARLOWE'S GHOST: Yet ambition hath begun with smaller things.

What seeks your company?

Power?

KARL: Representation.

MARLOWE: Fame?

NEIL: Preferably recognition before polling day.

MARLOWE: Dominion?

RODERICK: A functioning website would be nice.

MARLOWE: You are the strangest revolutionaries I have encountered.

Act XII

Enter Shakespeare

SHAKESPEARE: All politics is but a stage—

RODERICK: You didn't actually write that.

SHAKESPEARE: I might have.

NEIL: What counsel would you give?

SHAKESPEARE: Speak plainly.

For words become a fog when men employ
A thousand phrases to obscure one choice.

Yet guard against the equal opposite:

A question made too simple may conceal
The consequences living underneath.

Ask clearly.

Answer clearly.

Then debate the reasons honestly.

RODERICK: That's remarkably sensible.

SHAKESPEARE: I had four centuries to prepare.

Act XIII

Enter the Kiwi Jester

A trumpet attempts something magnificent but produces a squeak. Enter THE KIWI JESTER.

JESTER: Gidday!

So let me understand this.

You want seventy-one independents—

KARL: Yes.

JESTER: In Parliament.

NEIL: Yes.

JESTER: All thinking for themselves.

RODERICK: Yes.

JESTER: Mate, sometimes it's hard enough getting seventy-one New Zealanders to agree which fish-and-chip shop to use.

RODERICK: They don't need to agree.

JESTER: Oh!

Now you're talking.

You could have a proper Parliament.

Seventy-one people disagreeing honestly instead of six people disagreeing according to a timetable.

Laughter.

JESTER: And these seventy-one questions—

Who writes them?

RODERICK: We begin them.

The public improves them.

Candidates answer them.

JESTER: Question seventy-one better be:

“Do you support finishing this meeting before the pub shuts?”

AUDIENCE: YES!

JESTER: Landslide.

Act XIV

The Recorder

STEVE places a cellphone upon the lectern.

STEVE: Record.

BEEP.

RODERICK: Every appearance creates raw material.

A speech becomes audio.

Audio becomes transcript.

Transcript becomes article.

Article becomes archive.

Archive lets people compare what was said yesterday with what is said tomorrow.

NEIL: And if newspapers ignore us?

RODERICK: Publish it yourselves.

KARL: And if websites ignore us?

RODERICK: Build your own.

STEVE: And if nobody listens?

RODERICK: Then improve the speech.

Act XV

The Email to All

RODERICK sits before a glowing screen.

RODERICK: “To: Reply All.”

NEIL: Dangerous words.

RODERICK: Indeed.

KARL: What are you sending?

RODERICK: The questions.

NEIL: All seventy-one?

RODERICK: Eventually.

KARL: To whom?

RODERICK: Candidates.

Supporters.

Sceptics.

People who believe.

People who don't.

People who simply want to know what an MP would actually do when the bell rings and a vote is called.

NEIL: And what shall we call this document?

RODERICK thinks.

RODERICK: The Seventy-One Questions.

KARL: Conscience Questions?

RODERICK: Better.

THE SEVENTY-ONE CONSCIENCE QUESTIONS.

CHORUS: SEVENTY-ONE!

RODERICK: I knew that was coming.

Epilogue

The Question Returns to the Audience

All characters assemble.

NEIL: We began with a letter to a cousin.

KEVIN: An eighty-year-old declined.

JACKSON: A twenty-seven-year-old went back to painting fences.

STEVE: A meeting was held.

JACK: A badge was worn.

ROSETTA: A question was asked.

KARL: And an improbable political experiment continued.

RODERICK steps forward.

RODERICK: But mark this well.

No candidate owns the conscience of another.

No movement owns the voter.

No slogan substitutes for judgment.

Ask the questions.

Hear the answers.

Examine the reasons.

Check the evidence.

Record the promises.

And when polling day arrives—

the pencil belongs to you.

CHORUS OF SEVENTY-ONE QUESTIONS: Ask us!

PARTY REPRESENTATIVES: Answer us!

INDEPENDENTS: Question us!

AUDIENCE: Then let us decide.

The BULL enters.

BULL: Moooo.

RODERICK: And somebody please move the bull off the road.

BLACKOUT.

CURTAIN

A final voice from the darkness: “Is the cellphone still recording?”

STEVE: Yep.

VOICE: Good. We'll get an article out of that.
